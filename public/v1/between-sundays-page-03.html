Lost Is Not Alone

Genesis 28 begins with a traveler, a road, and a place easy to miss.

The scene

A traveler sleeps outside, using a stone for a pillow.

The line

Jacob wakes with a sentence he did not expect: surely.

Report / Ordinary Places DeskContinues page 04

Jacob leaves home with a story already behind him. He is not introduced to us as a polished spiritual hero on a retreat. He is moving because staying has become complicated. The road ahead is uncertain. The home behind him is wounded. Night comes before resolution does.

That is where Genesis 28 places the first sentence we are carrying through this issue: "I am with you." It arrives on the road, before Jacob has a settled address or a cleaned-up ending.

He stops in a place because the sun has gone down. The text does not describe a famous city or a carefully prepared room. It gives us a location ordinary enough to miss, then slows down long enough for a dream.

For readers who know what it is to live between decisions, between apologies, between jobs, between addresses, between Sunday morning certainty and Tuesday afternoon fatigue, that detail is worth noticing.

The dream is famous: a ladder or stairway, angels moving between heaven and earth, the Lord standing near, the old promise spoken again in a new place. The image is huge. The setting is not.

Jacob wakes up and names what he missed while he was asleep: "Surely the Lord is in this place, and I was not aware of it." The passage gives the reader the discovery before it gives a neat explanation.

Between Sundays begins here because the newspaper itself is meant to show up in ordinary places. A table. A seat. A lobby. A bag. A room where someone did not come looking for a sermon and found a story instead.

So this first issue stays close to the question in the passage: what places have we been moving through too quickly to notice?
